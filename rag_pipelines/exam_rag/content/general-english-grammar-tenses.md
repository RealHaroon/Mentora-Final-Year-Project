English verb tenses tell the reader *when* an action happens and whether
it's completed, ongoing, or habitual. The three main time categories —
past, present, and future — each split into four aspects: simple,
continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous.

- **Simple** states a plain fact or habitual action: "She studies every
  day" (present), "She studied yesterday" (past), "She will study tomorrow"
  (future).
- **Continuous** (also called progressive) describes an action in progress:
  "She is studying" (present), "She was studying" (past), "She will be
  studying" (future).
- **Perfect** describes an action completed before a reference point:
  "She has studied" (present perfect — completed at some point up to now),
  "She had studied" (past perfect — completed before another past event),
  "She will have studied" (future perfect — will be completed before a
  future point).
- **Perfect continuous** combines both ideas — an action that continued up
  to a reference point: "She has been studying for three hours."

**Active vs. passive voice** is a separate but related concept. In active
voice, the subject performs the action: "The teacher explained the lesson."
In passive voice, the subject receives the action, and the original subject
becomes optional: "The lesson was explained (by the teacher)." Passive
voice is formed with a form of "to be" + the past participle of the main
verb, and the tense of "to be" carries the same tense information as the
active sentence it was converted from.

**Common mistake to watch for:** mixing tenses within a single sentence
without a reason to — e.g. "I was walking to school and I see my friend"
should be "I was walking to school and I saw my friend," keeping both verbs
in the past tense since both actions happened at the same point in the
past.
