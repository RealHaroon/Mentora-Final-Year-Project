A good career transition roadmap breaks a long-term goal into a sequence
of smaller, concrete milestones rather than one vague target. A typical
6-month upskilling plan for a technical career transition (for example,
into Data Science) tends to follow this general shape:

**Months 1-2 — Fundamentals.** Build the baseline skills the target role
assumes everyone already has: programming fundamentals (Python is the
most common choice across data/ML-adjacent roles), basic statistics, and
comfort with the core tools of the field (SQL, version control). Skipping
this stage to jump straight to advanced topics is the most common mistake
— advanced material usually assumes fluency here that takes real
repetition to build.

**Month 3 — Core domain skills.** Move into the specific technical
methods the target role actually uses day to day — for a data-focused
role, this typically means classical machine learning algorithms
(regression, classification, clustering) using a library like
scikit-learn, learned through both structured courses and hands-on
practice on real (even if small) datasets.

**Month 4 — Specialization.** Go deeper into the subset of skills most
relevant to the specific target role — deep learning frameworks for ML
engineering, advanced SQL and visualization tools for analytics roles,
system design for software engineering.

**Month 5 — Portfolio / applied projects.** Build 1-3 real, complete
projects that demonstrate the skills above being applied to an actual
problem, not just completed tutorials. A small number of complete,
well-documented projects is consistently more convincing to employers
than a long list of half-finished ones.

**Month 6 — Job search readiness.** Mock interviews, resume/portfolio
polish, and applying — ideally starting this earlier than month 6 in
parallel with the later project work, since the job search process itself
(screening, interview rounds) takes real time once started.

**The most common roadmap mistake:** treating every month as equally
long — in practice, fundamentals take longer to solidify than people
expect, and rushing past them to reach "real" projects sooner usually
costs more time later than it saves.
