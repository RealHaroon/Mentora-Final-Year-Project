Physical chemistry applies physics concepts — energy, rates, equilibrium
— to understand how and why chemical reactions happen.

**Chemical kinetics (reaction rates).** The rate of a reaction depends on
how often and how effectively reactant particles collide — the
**collision theory**. Factors that increase reaction rate:
- **Concentration**: more particles per volume means more frequent
  collisions.
- **Temperature**: particles move faster and collide harder, and more
  collisions exceed the minimum energy needed to react (the **activation
  energy**).
- **Surface area**: more exposed surface (e.g. powder vs. a solid lump)
  means more collision opportunities.
- **Catalysts**: provide an alternative reaction pathway with lower
  activation energy, speeding up the reaction without being consumed.

**Chemical equilibrium.** In a reversible reaction, equilibrium is
reached when the forward and reverse reaction rates become equal — not
when the amounts of reactants and products are equal. **Le Chatelier's
Principle** predicts how equilibrium shifts when disturbed: if you
increase the concentration of a reactant, raise pressure (for gas
reactions with fewer moles on one side), or change temperature, the
equilibrium shifts in the direction that partially counteracts the
change.

**Thermodynamics basics.** A reaction is **exothermic** if it releases
heat to the surroundings (products have less energy than reactants);
**endothermic** if it absorbs heat (products have more energy). This is
often shown on an energy profile diagram, with activation energy as the
"hill" the reactants must climb before reaching products.

**Moles and stoichiometry**: the mole is a counting unit (6.022 × 10²³
particles, Avogadro's number) that lets chemists relate the mass of a
substance to the actual number of particles/reacting ratios in a
balanced chemical equation.
