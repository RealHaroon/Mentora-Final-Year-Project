Modern physics covers the developments from the early 20th century
onward that revealed the limits of classical (Newtonian) physics at very
small scales and very high speeds — mainly quantum mechanics and
relativity, plus atomic and nuclear structure.

**Atomic structure.** An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged
**nucleus** (protons and neutrons) surrounded by electrons occupying
discrete, quantized **energy levels** (shells), not arbitrary orbits.
Electrons can jump between levels by absorbing or emitting a photon with
energy exactly equal to the gap between levels (E = hf, where h is
Planck's constant and f is the photon's frequency) — this is the basis of
atomic emission/absorption spectra, which are unique to each element.

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous emission of particles/energy from an
unstable nucleus:
- **Alpha decay**: emits a helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons); mass
  number drops by 4, atomic number by 2.
- **Beta decay**: a neutron converts to a proton (emitting an electron),
  or vice versa; mass number unchanged, atomic number shifts by 1.
- **Gamma decay**: emits high-energy electromagnetic radiation, no change
  in mass or atomic number — just a release of excess nuclear energy.

**Half-life** is the time for half of a radioactive sample to decay — a
constant property of each isotope, independent of the sample's size.

**Einstein's photoelectric effect** showed that light behaves as discrete
packets of energy (photons), not just a continuous wave — only light
above a minimum (threshold) frequency can eject electrons from a metal
surface, regardless of how intense the light is, which classical wave
theory couldn't explain. This dual wave-particle behavior of light (and,
by extension, matter) is the foundation of quantum mechanics.
