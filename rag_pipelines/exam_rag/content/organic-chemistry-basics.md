Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds —
carbon's ability to form four stable covalent bonds, including long
chains and rings with itself, makes an enormous diversity of compounds
possible, from simple fuels to DNA.

**Hydrocarbons** (compounds of only carbon and hydrogen) are classified
by their bonding:
- **Alkanes** (single bonds only, e.g. methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆) —
  "saturated," relatively unreactive, general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
- **Alkenes** (contain at least one C=C double bond, e.g. ethene C₂H₄) —
  "unsaturated," more reactive because the double bond can readily break
  open for addition reactions. General formula CₙH₂ₙ.
- **Alkynes** (contain a C≡C triple bond, e.g. ethyne C₂H₂) — even more
  reactive than alkenes.

**Functional groups** are specific atom groupings that give a molecule
characteristic chemical behavior, regardless of the rest of the carbon
chain:
- **–OH** (hydroxyl): alcohols, e.g. ethanol.
- **–COOH** (carboxyl): carboxylic acids, e.g. acetic acid.
- **–NH₂** (amine): found in amino acids.
- **C=O** (carbonyl): aldehydes and ketones.

**Isomerism**: compounds with the identical molecular formula but
different structural arrangements, giving them different physical/
chemical properties — e.g. butane and isobutane both have the formula
C₄H₁₀ but different branching.

**Polymers** are large molecules built from many repeating smaller units
(monomers) joined by covalent bonds — e.g. many ethene monomers joining
to form polyethylene plastic. Proteins (from amino acid monomers) and
starch (from glucose monomers) are biological polymers.
